Cryptomnesia

The design board. The game itself now runs as an app from the same engine and page recipes; this page stays as the place to react to art and words. Latest: all thirty-two new plates redrawn after the first pass. They are now built as boxes in real space, so walls, doors and objects share their vanishing points. Lamps are small and tight and the rooms are lit by contrast, not by a wash of colour. The works runs on copper light; the level on orange, red and grey-blue; the basement on tungsten. Still marked new.

The way in

Three questions pick where you start

Answer them to try it. Each disposition opens on a different page and bends the story a little. The plate on each card is what the “You are…” screen shows.

Proposed shape, about 60 pages

Branch, meet, narrow, fan out

Dusk · 8

Arrive from one of four entry pages. Two small choices that set flags and are echoed later. No deaths yet.

Night · 30

Four districts, room to see three. Each sets one flag. Deaths here are short, warned, and worth finding.

Small hours · 10

Everyone passes through the same scenes, altered by flags. Choices drop from three to two to one.

Endings · 6 + 1

Mixed outcomes built from flags, not win or lose. One hidden ending that no choice links to.

Panel rhythm. First pages and endings get one or two panels, so they land hard. Everything between gets three to eight. The text on a page notices one thing per panel, so a busier page reads slower and closer. The five and six panel pages are where the worst things are, usually in the smallest box.

About six more pages go to deaths. This is Ashwell's branch-and-bottleneck with a late fan-out. Three or four flags carry the memory.

House rules for the telling

What the pictures do that the words don't

  • Silent panels. On most pages one or two panels are never mentioned. The house, the car, the kitchen you walked through. They set the place. The text talks about the porch, the yard, the window.
  • No picture twice. A detail gets one panel. If something crosses the floor it is a strip along the foot of the page, wherever the text is looking.
  • Mostly stills. Two or three panels on a page move. The rest hold still, which makes the moving ones worse.
  • Three signs. They recur, hand-drawn and never quite the same: painted on a barn, cut into a lawn, chalked in a loft, on a card, on a screen for one frame. Nobody explains them. People in town act as if you already know.
  • Type follows the track. Grenze Gotisch for the Watcher and Keeper tracks, MedievalSharp for the Knocker and Stray, and Pirata One on a rare page where the town itself is speaking.
  • Old machines. A set left on, a recorder whose clock was never set, a tape with your handwriting on it. They are things you can operate: "Hit play on the VCR" is a choice.
  • Every ending is dark. No rescue, no waking up. The best ones take something the reader was sure of and turn it over: you were never the visitor, the tape is ahead of you, the voice is borrowed.
  • Endings are vignettes. They sink into black. The exception is a violent death, which gets a hard box so there is nowhere to look away to.

Storylines

Nine Lamps · my pick

You are the new lamplighter of Dunmarrow. There is oil for six lamps. There are nine streets.

Premise
A street left dark is gone by morning: its houses, its people, and everyone's memory of them. Each of three nights you choose the round.
Why it fits
The orange splash is the mechanic. A lit page has its accent. A forgotten street's page has none. Then a wrong-coloured window appears on a street you let go two nights ago.
Memory
Nine lamp flags. People you met vanish from later pages and from the captions. The map page redraws around your choices.
Endings
Dawn for six. The long dark. The keeper. The wrong street. Hidden: a tenth lamp that shows for one frame on the map page.
First page
The oil can is lighter than they said it would be. Marrow Street is first on the round. Someone is already standing under the lamp. Light it · Walk on to Tanner's Row · Ask who's there

Low Water

Once a year the tide at Sallow Quay goes out too far. Tonight it shows the roofs.

Premise
Under the harbour is an older town, street for street the same as the one above, including your house with a light on. You have twelve moves before the tide turns.
Why it fits
Every upper-town page has a drowned twin: same panels, different palette, wrong accent. Readers compare the pair to find what changed.
Memory
A visible tide clock, and three things you can carry up or leave below.
Endings
Caught by the tide. Home, but it's the copy. The bell brought up. Staying below. Hidden: never go down.
First page
The boat is where the water was. Beyond it, wet slate, a chimney, a weathervane you know. Walk out · Fetch your brother first · Watch from the wall

Two Hundred and Twelve

You are sent to count the people of Wick St Agnes. The last census says 212. Every house has one more than it should.

Premise
Door to door, kitchen to kitchen. At each table sits someone the family insists has always been there. A name struck in red is gone on the next page.
Why it fits
Bone interiors, and the red is your ink. Multi-panel pages suit it: one box per face at the table, and one of them never moves.
Memory
One number, the count.
Endings
By final count. 212 exactly. Under. Over. 213, where the last line is you. Hidden: zero.
First page
Mrs Pell sets out five cups. The form says four. Nobody at the table looks at the fifth chair. Write four · Write five · Ask the child to name everyone

The Sitting

Your grandmother is laid out in the front room. Someone must sit with her until first light. The rules are pinned to the door.

Premise
Seven rules. Each hour a relative goes home and one rule is tested. One of the seven was not written by the family.
Why it fits
Smallest to build: one house, and each window is a panel showing the town or the woods in its own palette.
Memory
Which rules you've kept. Breaking one is always warned and not always fatal.
Endings
One for each broken rule. First light. Hidden: keep all seven, including the false one.
First page
Aunt Ro puts her coat on. "You'll be fine. Just do what the paper says." The paper has seven lines in two different hands. Read the rules · Count the chairs · Ask whose writing that is

How I'd build it, and what I need from you

Each card is a decision. Keep the option you want in each group and drop the others.

Framework

Vite + React · my pick

The whole game runs in the browser: the art engine, the story state, the wallet. A static build needs no server and can be pinned to IPFS, which suits a fully on-chain piece.

Next.js

Worth it only if you want each page to have its own link preview image, which needs a server to render. Otherwise it adds hosting you don't need.

Format, measured on the first eleven pages

APNG, with a plain PNG inside it

A page uses four colours, so it packs as a 2-bit indexed PNG. An APNG is that same PNG with extra frames attached for only the panels that move. Every current browser plays it. Anything that cannot play it still shows the first frame, because the file is a valid PNG. A GIF cannot do better here and is usually larger; I have not measured GIF.

Still page
Mean 4.7 KB.
Animated page
Mean 17.9 KB. This is an upper bound: it stores every frame of each moving panel whole, with no frame-to-frame differencing.
Sixty pages
About 1.1 MB animated, or 280 KB as stills, if the rest of the book averages like these eleven.
Not yet tested
Whether the marketplaces and wallets you care about animate APNG or show only the first frame. Words set into the art are not in these figures; they add little.
Putting the art on-chain

Store each page as an APNG · my pick on an L2

What is minted is the picture itself. Nothing has to run for it to display, and the thumbnail problem from last round goes away. About 1 MB for the book, which at 200 gas per byte is roughly 200 million gas: fine on an L2, very heavy on mainnet.

Store the renderer and recipes · my pick on mainnet

The engine goes in the contract once (about 70 KB of source today) and each page is a recipe of under 1 KB. Roughly a tenth of the bytes. The token draws itself as a live HTML document, with a still PNG stored alongside for thumbnails. New art means the stored engine can never change, so the engine has to be finished first.

Chain

Ethereum mainnet

The most durable home and the one collectors rank highest. Storing code costs 200 gas per byte, so the engine and sixty recipes is a real deployment bill.

An L2 such as Base

Same contract, far cheaper to deploy and to mint. A 0.003 ETH mint is not eaten by fees.

Wallets and minting
Token
I'm reading "erc-1115" as ERC-1155. One token id per page, 0.003 ETH, minted from a button on the page you are reading. Pages are 360 by 480.
No keys
wagmi and viem with browser-wallet discovery (MetaMask, Rabby, Rainbow and others) and Coinbase Wallet need no API key. Chain reads go through public RPC endpoints, which are rate-limited.
WalletConnect
It cannot run without a project ID from Reown. The ID is free and public, but it is a keyed service. I'd wire it so it switches on only if you supply an ID, and the site works without it.
To decide
Can anyone mint any page, or only pages they have reached? Is each page's edition open forever, capped, or timed? Where do proceeds go?
